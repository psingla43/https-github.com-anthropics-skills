Separation Agreements · Ontario

Settle the terms once, in writing, so you can both move on.

A separation agreement records how you and your spouse will handle parenting, support, and property after you separate. Done properly, it lets you resolve these questions between yourselves — without asking a court to decide them. This page explains what an agreement covers and what makes one hold up.

A separation agreement is a distinct step from a divorce. Many couples put an agreement in place first, which is often what keeps a later divorce uncontested.

What it covers

The questions a separation agreement can settle.

An agreement can address some or all of the issues below. Which ones apply depends on whether you have children, own property, or owe support.

01

Parenting

Decision-making responsibility and parenting time — where the children live, how time is shared, and how major decisions are made.

02

Child support

The amount and schedule of support, generally set with reference to the child support guidelines, and how expenses are shared.

03

Spousal support

Whether spousal support is payable, in what amount, and for how long — or a clear statement that neither spouse will claim it.

04

Property & debts

How you divide property and debts, including bank accounts, investments, pensions, vehicles, and what happens with the matrimonial home.

What makes it hold up

An agreement is only worth what it can withstand later.

A separation agreement can be challenged if it was not made properly. A few things matter a great deal to whether it stands.

  1. Full financial disclosure

    Each spouse should exchange complete, honest information about income, property, and debts. An agreement built on hidden or incomplete disclosure is far more vulnerable to being set aside.

  2. Independent legal advice

    Each spouse should have their own lawyer review the agreement before signing. This is one of the strongest protections — it shows each person understood the agreement and signed it freely.

  3. No pressure

    The agreement should be entered voluntarily, without undue pressure or a rushed deadline. Terms that are grossly one-sided can also draw scrutiny.

  4. Properly signed and witnessed

    The agreement must be in writing, signed, and witnessed as required. The formalities are not a technicality — they are part of what makes it enforceable.

Common questions

Questions we are asked most often.

General answers to help you understand how separation agreements work. For advice on your own situation, please speak with us directly.

Do we need a separation agreement to be separated?

No. You are separated when the marriage or relationship has ended and you begin living separate and apart — an agreement is not what creates the separation. What an agreement does is record how you will handle parenting, support, and property, so those questions are settled and in writing rather than left open.

Is a separation agreement the same as a divorce?

No. They are different things. A divorce legally ends the marriage; a separation agreement settles the terms of your separation. Common-law couples, who do not divorce, may still need a separation agreement. Many married couples do both — the agreement first, the divorce afterward.

Can we write our own agreement?

You can, but there are real risks. An agreement made without full disclosure or independent legal advice is more likely to be challenged and set aside later. A home-made agreement that misses an issue, or is worded unclearly, can create the very dispute it was meant to prevent. Having each spouse independently advised is a modest cost against that risk.

Can a separation agreement be changed later?

Sometimes. Spouses can agree to change an agreement, and certain terms — particularly those affecting children, such as support and parenting — can be revisited if circumstances change significantly. Property terms are generally harder to reopen. What can be changed, and how, depends on the agreement and the facts.

What is "independent legal advice," and why does each of us need it?

It means each spouse has their own lawyer explain the agreement — what it says, what rights are being given up, and whether it is reasonable — before signing. It protects both people: it helps ensure each understood and agreed freely, and it makes the agreement much harder to challenge afterward.
